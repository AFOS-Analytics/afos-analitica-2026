## What moved, and what only appeared to move

The Senate's 6.00pp did not spread across the week: **5.00pp came on Aug 11** and **1.00pp on Aug 12**. From Wednesday on the price stood still. Over the same stretch the Republican side of the House did not move **a hundredth**.

📌 **The asymmetry is the finding, not the move.** Both contracts are sensitive to the same national mood, and the House is usually the more reactive instrument, because it is 435 simultaneous races. This week the Senate moved six times as much. A national shock does not produce that shape. A set of specific results does.

And what happened on Aug 11 is record, not inference: it was primary Tuesday, and our own press collection from that day carries **NPR** and **The New York Times**, independent of each other, covering the Republican Senate special primary in **South Carolina**, which ended undecided and went to a runoff. The same night, in **Wisconsin**, the polling favourite lost the primary for governor.

⛔ **This brief does not claim the price rose because of those results.** What it claims is what can be checked: the Senate price repriced on the day Senate-relevant results arrived, and the House price did not follow in the same proportion.

## The average rose, and no voter was asked

Over the same stretch the AFOS generic ballot average went from **D+5.66** to **D+6.20**.

The detail that changes the whole reading: **the most recent field date across the entire base is Aug 04**, and it was Aug 04 at the start of the week too. No new round entered. What changed was the denominator, because the 30-day window rolled and the set fell from **29 polls to 20**, with pollsters going from 23 to 16.

📌 **Nine polls dropped off the edge, and the average rose 0.54pp because of it.** Anyone treating that number as movement in vote intention is reading window arithmetic as if it were the electorate.

⚠️ This is the kind of defect no validator catches, because the value is correct. **What is missing is not accuracy, it is the label.** That is why the grid row and the summary card in this issue both say the variation came from the edge.

## What the seats distribution says, and why it is checkable

The Republican Senate seats book summed to **100.30%** at capture and stayed inside the gate in **every** reading of the last 24 hours. It publishes a number, and what it says is:

- **51.50%** for the Republicans finishing with **49 seats or fewer**, which is a Democratic majority
- **12.50%** for **exactly 50**, which is a tie
- **36.30%** for **51 or more**, which is a Republican majority

⭐ **The binary control contract pays 52.50% for the Democrats.** The two books are independent, traded by different people, and they agree to within about a point. Coherence between a band book and its matching binary is guaranteed by no construction at all, and it is worth publishing when it shows up.

It is worth more still because **the equivalent House book does not have that property**: it summed to **107.40%** at capture and **108%** in the series, falls outside the gate and therefore enters this issue as shape only, with no number. The state governors book summed to 96.40% at capture, which would clear, but it swung from **89% to 99% across seven readings inside 24 hours**, with four inside the range, and it does not publish either. The popular vote margin book summed to **153.60%** and remains failed, as in every earlier issue.

## What weakens this reading, and it is declared

**The polling ruler has been frozen for thirteen days.** With the most recent field date at Aug 04, any comparison between price and poll in this issue uses one live side and one frozen side. That does not invalidate the measurement, but it changes what it supports: we can say the price moved and the poll brought no new information, and we cannot say the price moved away from the poll.

**The Senate series is thirty days long and the House series twenty.** Every superlative in this issue says "of the series", with the start date, and none says "of the cycle". Both series begin where collection was switched on, not where the contest began.

**The two sides of a single contract do not add to exactly 100%.** The House closes at 100.00% and the Senate at 101.00% at this issue's capture. That is binary book spread, it is small and it is visible, unlike what happens inside band books.

## Where the previous issue framed it wrong

**Issue №3**, on Aug 10, listed as its base scenario "Republicans hold the Senate", at **54.50%**, and as its against-the-pricing scenario "Democrats flip the Senate", at **46.50%**.

Seven days later those two numbers are all but swapped: **48.50%** and **52.50%**.

📌 **It was not a misreading of the price.** The base scenario correctly described what the book was paying at that moment. What the week showed is something else, and it is the transferable lesson: **a 54.50% price in a binary contract is a weak claim, and it carries no mark saying it is weak.** Whoever reads the number alone receives it in the same typeface as a 95% price.

It is the same reason this issue publishes the seats distribution next to the binary, the 9.00pp spread between pollsters next to the average, and the 24-hour swing range next to each book's sum. **The single number is the product that hides its own uncertainty most**, and this brief's job is to give the uncertainty back to the page.
