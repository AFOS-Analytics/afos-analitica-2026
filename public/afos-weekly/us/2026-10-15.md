howToRead:
  title: "The likely-voter screen, and why it can move a poll either way"
  text: "Every pollster this close to an election has to decide who will show up. A **registered-voter** sample asks everyone on the rolls. A **likely-voter** sample keeps only those the firm judges will actually vote, using questions about past turnout, intention and interest. That screen is the firm's own model, not a measurement, and the two electorates are not the same people: one that favours high-interest voters helps whichever side is angrier this year. Across our window the lean is mild, likely-voter rounds running **0.92** of a point below registered-voter rounds. The reading for a voter: behind one number there is often a second, and the gap is a modelling choice the headline does not show."

methodology: "Market prices in this issue are October 15 closes, the last point recorded that day in our daily database backup, compared with the October 8 closes that Issue No. 10 published. Both ends of each binary pair are printed raw and normalised, because a pair that does not sum to 100 moves one side without the other side trading. The polling average is a simple, unweighted mean of one round per polling firm per wave over a rolling thirty-day window, with the hierarchy likely voters over registered voters over adults when a firm publishes more than one row in the aggregating index. When a firm publishes a second screen that the index does not tabulate, we keep the tabulated row and record the other one in our source-verification file rather than ingesting it, because ingesting it would change the provenance of the served number. We never subtract a market price from a polling margin: one is a probability of controlling a chamber and the other is a margin in vote share, and in 2012 Democrats won more House votes and fewer seats."
