howToRead:
  title: "What one election can and cannot say about a price"
  text: "A control price is a probability, and the contract settles yes or no. On the eve the Senate price was **76.50%**. Had Republicans kept the Senate, that fact alone would not have made the price wrong, and Democrats winning does not make it right. Prices are judged over many events, by checking whether things priced near 75% happen about three times in four. Fourteen issues hold one outcome per chamber, and a dated record, written before the vote, that anyone can check."

methodology: "Market prices run from the October 29 close, the last point recorded that day in our daily database backup, to settlement: the House contract settled on November 4 at 13:34 UTC, and the Senate and schedule contracts earlier that morning. The eve record is the reading certified on November 2 at 22:06 UTC by two captures eight minutes apart that agreed within 0.20pp; on election night the Senate book failed that test four times before passing at 01:52 UTC, and only certified readings are printed. New money is measured from the October 29 close to the final traded volume. The seat-market bands are read at the November 2 close and used only when they sum to between 95% and 105%. The polling average is a simple, unweighted mean of one round per polling firm per wave over a thirty-day window, with the hierarchy likely voters over registered voters over adults, and it counts only the question that offers party labels: rounds that name each respondent's district candidates, or that ask which party should control Congress, are listed in the data file and left out. From this issue the window ends on election day, November 3, so the average no longer moves as rounds age out. We never subtract a market price from a polling margin: one is a probability of controlling a chamber and the other is a margin in vote share."
